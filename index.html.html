01 — The Study

Following the money from budget line to barangay street.

Disaster preparedness is financed before anything happens. This study looks at that quiet part of local governance: the fund every local government unit is required to keep for disasters, what the law says it must pay for, and whether the public record lets a resident follow the peso.

Reference base: the DRRM Act and its implementing rules, Commission on Audit guidance, the Ombudsman Act, and the 1987 Constitution.

Focus questions

01

How is the fund allocated?

Which portion of the local budget is set aside for disaster preparedness, and how is the barangay share computed, released, and carried over?

02

What records should show where it went?

Which plans, vouchers, procurement documents, and reports are required to trace an expenditure from approval to delivery?

03

Who can actually see it?

How visible is that paper trail to the residents it protects — before a disaster, not after the audit?

02 — Fund Flow

Where the money is supposed to go.

Republic Act No. 10121 does not leave the local disaster fund to discretion. It sets the size of the fund, splits it between standing response money and preparedness work, and tells the local government unit what to do with whatever is left over.

03 — Accountability

Three offices stand behind every receipt.

A disaster fund is only as strong as the accountability that surrounds it. These are the three authorities the study reads it against.

Constitutional audit

Commission on Audit

The COA audits every peso that passes through government. Its mandate lets it examine records, disallow irregular expenditures, and publish what it finds — which is why its guidance anchors this study.

COA mandate

Investigative power

Office of the Ombudsman

Republic Act No. 6770 created the Office of the Ombudsman to investigate and prosecute public officers whose acts betray the public trust.

RA 6770

Public disclosure

The 1987 Constitution

Article II, Section 28 adopts a policy of full public disclosure of all transactions involving public interest, and Article XI, Section 1 reminds every officer that public office is a public trust.

1987 Constitution
